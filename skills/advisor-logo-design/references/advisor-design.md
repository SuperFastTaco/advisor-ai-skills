# Advisor Design Guidance

Use the practice's real positioning and clients to choose design directions. These are creative starting points, not universal financial-industry rules or claims about how colors affect people.

## Understand the practice

- **Retirement-income focus:** The design can emphasize clarity, continuity, and an approachable planning relationship. Use only the advisor's actual services; do not imply guaranteed outcomes.
- **Investment/wealth management:** Consider the firm's preference for discretion, organization, or continuity. "Wealth" does not automatically imply ornamental luxury.
- **Comprehensive planning:** A connected or coordinated visual idea may fit a practice that actually coordinates several areas of a client's financial life.
- **Insurance-oriented practice:** Relationship and protection themes may be relevant. Do not portray the practice as a carrier or add insurer marks without supplied authorization.
- **Business-owner, family, or community niche:** Draw from the supplied audience, name story, and distinctive service approach. A founder's personal taste is one input; the intended client experience matters too.

These categories can overlap. A job title, website title, or logo cannot establish registration status, fee model, fiduciary status, tax/legal qualifications, or affiliation.

## Editable starting directions

| Direction | Proposed impression | Possible visual approach |
|---|---|---|
| Reassuring retirement practice | Calm, clarity, approachability | Open forms, readable type, restrained mark |
| Established wealth practice | Continuity, discretion, refinement | Intentional wordmark or a simple monogram |
| Modern planning practice | Organization, progress, accessibility | Clear typography, useful geometry, flexible layouts |
| Community or family practice | Warmth, relationships, familiarity | Softer forms, inviting typography, name-driven symbolism |

Use an existing approved palette and typography unless the requested redesign includes revising them. When proposing a new palette, present it as a choice with readable contrast and practical one-color reproduction. Navy and gold are options, not a mandatory finance uniform.

## Make the concepts distinct

Consider three different structures when the brief allows:

1. A wordmark whose spacing, weight, and typography fit the practice's name.
2. A monogram using the actual supplied initials, not a stock letter or unrelated acronym.
3. A combination mark with a simple shape that reflects a real name story, audience, or positioning idea.

Use only the applicable directions; do not force a symbol when a strong wordmark works better. Give each concept a clear reason to exist. A color swap does not constitute a new concept.

Literal shields, pillars, leaves, mountains, compasses, bridges, and upward charts are familiar starting symbols. Use them only when the meaning is relevant and the execution distinctive. Avoid automatic rising-profit imagery and invented badges. Do not add a tagline, certification, "fee-only," "fiduciary," "guaranteed," or another business assertion without supported wording.

## Judge the actual design

- Does the mark fit the stated audience and relationship, and use the exact firm name?
- Can the full name be read in the intended website header or stationery use?
- Does the mark remain recognizable at the smallest tested size? Record the size and the variant tested; do not invent a universal minimum.
- Can a clean one-color version reproduce the identity without relying on gradients, shine, tiny gaps, or elaborate linework?
- Are layouts useful for the stated applications? Pen decoration and square avatars often need different variants.
- Are shapes, proportions, spacing, and lettering consistent across versions?
- Is transparency real, and are dark-background variants actually legible?
- Are exact colors from a supplied specification distinguished from approximate sampled colors?

Inspect image-generation results as concepts. For final typography use an actual named font with known usage rights or properly constructed lettering when possible. Record unresolved licensing rather than promising commercial font rights. For a production vector, verify editable paths/text or intentionally outlined lettering; embedded raster imagery is not a vector mark.

Mockups demonstrate use. They do not establish that a printer, embroiderer, compliance team, or trademark reviewer has accepted the artwork.
